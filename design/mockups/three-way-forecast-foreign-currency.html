FOUR RULINGS FROM MIKE, 2026-09-26, and the drawing shows each one. (1) The advisor enters the supplier's invoice in its own currency with an assumed exchange rate; each payment converts at that rate and becomes the stock's cost. (2) Freight and duty join the stock's cost and are charged as the stock sells, not in full in the month it lands. (3) Overseas sales are entered in the customer's currency with an assumed rate; an exchange gain or loss arises only on money not yet collected. (4) Both 10% allowances stop being a cost. Each becomes a “what if the rate moves” setting whose effect is shown, not charged. The accounting basis for all four is recorded in design/CALCULATION-ASSUMPTIONS.md §1 (NZ IAS 21, NZ IFRIC 22, NZ IAS 2).

Why it works this way

Each payment to a supplier is converted at the rate on the day it is paid, and money paid before the goods arrive becomes part of what the stock cost. A rate move only matters for money not yet paid or collected, which is what the tiles above show.

Six questions to rule, one at a time

  1. The rate is written “1 NZD buys 0.6000 USD”, the way NZ banks quote it. Recommended: it is the figure an advisor reads off a bank screen.
  2. One supplier currency and one customer currency per forecast, not one per shipment. Recommended: the workbook has one currency per supplier sheet; a second currency is a later request.
  3. The “Exchange-rate movement” line leaves the profit and loss and the cash flow. With one assumed rate it is always zero, so the risk lives in the what-if tiles. This reverses part of the 2026-09-04 ruling that made it its own direct-cost line, so it is yours to decide.
  4. Both what-if settings default to 10%, the same figure the allowances use today.
  5. Stock already on the water at the opening date stays in NZD: its deposit is already paid, and its balance is entered as an NZD amount. Recommended for now. The unpaid balance is genuinely exposed to the rate; modelling that is a later request.
