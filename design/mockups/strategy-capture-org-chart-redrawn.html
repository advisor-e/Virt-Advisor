What he said, having used it

  • “no ability to enter their role which is critical in an organisational chart” — he typed billy smith, bobby moors and jason gunn into the only text box there is, the one headed Role. There was nowhere to say what they do.
  • “1 person does not have to report to anyone - nil needs to be an option” — it already was one, and the word for it was hiding. The empty choice read “Reports to…”, which is a prompt telling you to pick somebody, not a choice meaning nobody.
  • “it looks boring - lacks colour and is not engaging - looks very cheap” — the old drawing used one accent and pale grey rules on white. Of the brand palette — Navy, Blue, Cyan, Sky — it used navy for text and blue for a single border.

What that produced on screen, measured

  • Three people, no jobs. The chart he built said billy smith reports to bobby moors. It could not say either of them was a Managing Director.
  • A 176 px-wide chart in a 1,000 px panel — the box sized itself to the panel, not to the drawing, so most of it was empty white.
  • Every row double height, because the voice bar sits above the box rather than beside it. Three roles filled the screen.
  • Nothing told him which way was up. Every box was the same pale outline, so the top of the chart looked exactly like the bottom of it.

Five decisions

Each with the recommendation and the argument against it, as on the drawing this replaces, and each ruling kept beside them so the build can be laid against this page afterwards and every difference named. Asked one at a time. Ruling these is not approval to build from this page — that is its own question.

A · Three columns, or two with the person inside the role box?

An org chart names a position and the person in it. His sheet has one column and he filled it with people, because that was the only box on the screen.

Recommended: three columns — Role, Name, Reporting Head. A role and a person are two facts, and the chart box shows both because a client recognises the names. Two facts in one box cannot be sorted, reported on, or left half-filled for a vacancy.

Against: it is a third column on a table that was two, and his own sheet has neither of the first two headed.

☑ RULED: the table has THREE columns — Role, Name, Reporting Head.Mike, 2026‑09‑21. As recommended. ⚠ Name is therefore the SECOND word on this screen that is ours rather than his — Role was the first, ruled the same day.

B · Must every role have a person in it?

A client redesigning their structure has positions before they have people, and an advisor sitting with them will draw the shape first.

Recommended: no. Name is optional, and a role with nobody in it prints the role alone — no word like “vacant” is invented for it.

Against: a chart of empty boxes tells you less, and nothing on screen will then say whether a blank means unfilled or unanswered.

☑ RULED: Name is OPTIONAL. A role with nobody in it is drawn, with the job title alone and no invented word beneath it.Mike, 2026‑09‑21. As recommended. ⚠ The argument against stands and is not solved by this ruling: a blank Name still cannot say whether the position is unfilled or simply not yet typed. Nothing on the screen distinguishes them, and nothing pretends to.

C · What the “reports to nobody” choice is called

It already exists and already works. It reads “Reports to…”, which is a prompt telling you to choose somebody — which is exactly why he could not find it.

Recommended: call it Nobody, and make it what a new role already says rather than something you must go and select. The word appears in a list of people’s names, so it reads as one of the choices.

Against: “Nobody” is blunt, and it is a word of ours on a screen where the third column heading is his.

☑ RULED: the choice reads Nobody, and it is what a new role STARTS on rather than something to go and select.Mike, 2026‑09‑21. As recommended, and asked as a wording question rather than decided. ⚠ THE OPTION WAS NEVER MISSING — it worked before this ruling and the word was the whole defect. A screen can hide a thing it already does.

D · Does Reporting Head name the ROLE or the PERSON?

With two columns there is now something to choose between. His own sheet names roles — Board, CEO — and his worked example has no people in it at all.

Recommended: the role. It is what his document does; it survives a person leaving; and it is already what the built code stores, so nothing captured so far is lost.

Against: in the room an advisor talks about people, not positions, and will look for a name in that list.

☑ RULED: Reporting Head lists ROLES. The chart is built out of positions, and a person leaving changes one name rather than breaking the structure.Mike, 2026‑09‑21. As recommended. ⚠ The argument against is real and is only half answered: the chart BOX carries both lines, so the pairing is visible once built — but in the dropdown itself an advisor thinking about a person still has to translate to their title. Nothing here removes that step.

E · Colour the chart by level

On the old drawing every box was the same pale outline, so the top of the chart looked exactly like the bottom. Depth is the one thing an org chart exists to show.

Recommended: colour each level, Navy at the top through to Sky at the front line. Every colour is already in BRAND-TOKENS.md. It is information, not decoration: seven levels become readable without tracing a line.

Against: seven bands is a lot of colour on one screen, and a plan printed in black and white loses the distinction entirely — the lines still carry it, but the effect goes.

☑ RULED: the chart is COLOURED BY LEVEL, Navy at the top through to Sky at the front line.Mike, 2026‑09‑21. As recommended, and it is the ruling that answers what he actually asked for — “it looks boring - lacks colour and is not engaging - looks very cheap”. ⚠ THE PRINTED COST IS ACCEPTED, NOT SOLVED: a client’s plan printed in black and white loses every band. The connecting lines still carry the structure so nothing becomes wrong, but the thing that makes seven levels readable on screen is the first thing a photocopier takes away.
