What this is

Item 4.90 — the Retirement Review screen, drawn for approval on 2026-09-13. THE SCREEN IS NOT BUILT. What is built and pushed: the maths (server/report/retirementReviewModel.js), its golden test — 47 cases, eight mutations run and all eight caught — and the route POST /api/report/retirement-review.

Every number on this page is real. It is the sample in your own Exposure.Retirement.Review (1).xlsx put through that model. Nothing is invented to fill a space. Where a figure differs from the spreadsheet, step 4 says so on the screen itself.

The Model Library card still says "coming soon" on purpose. Turning it on is what pulls the report into the frame guard, which then requires this page to exist. It flips when this is built.

The look is not a question for you. Colours, spacing, cards, the dark banner and the step chips are read straight off Quick Position, the Loan Estimator and the High Level Budget, per your ruling of 2026-07-23. What is a question is every word — the step names, the field labels and, above all, the verdict sentence on step 4. Those are marked as you go.

Why four steps

The workbook has four visible sheets and two hidden mortgage sheets. The four fall into four different conversations, which is what the steps follow:

  • Step 1 — the conversation. The Quick Calculator sheet. Ten questions about what the client wants retirement to be, and the savings gap that answers them.
  • Step 2 — what they have. Income, cash, superannuation, the government pension, other investments, the business.
  • Step 3 — the properties. Up to six, each with its mortgage, its growth and the year it is sold, if it is.
  • Step 4 — the next twenty years. The projection and the verdict.

Step 1 stands alone, and that is deliberate. The Quick Calculator shares no figure with the other three — the workbook keeps it on its own sheet for the same reason. An adviser can run step 1 in a first meeting, before a single balance is known, and stop there. Recommended: keep it as step 1 rather than a separate model, so one card in the library covers the whole conversation.

Step 1 — the wording to settle

The four free-text questions are the workbook's own, shortened. Its wording is "What does retirement mean to you?", "What would you hate to have to give up?", "What are you most looking forward to?" and "Do you have provisions in place that remove the need for this?" — the fourth is the only one reworded here, because the original does not say what "this" is.

The headline sentence is the decision on this step. Drawn as "The gap is $14,687 a month". Recommended because it is the one number the client will remember and the sheet exists to produce it. The alternative reading — "You need $1,532,871 at 65" — is the same fact from the other end.

"Finish here" ends the model at step 1 and produces a one-page result. That is the point of the sheet standing alone; without the button, an adviser who only wanted the gap has to abandon the screen.

Step 3 — the wording to settle

Plain words are used for the mortgage columns — Worth, Owing, Mortgage cost, Left over — rather than Value, Debt, Servicing, Surplus. Recommended because this screen is shown to the client, not just the adviser. The workbook's own headings are the second set.

The three mortgage types keep the workbook's names — Table, Reducing, Interest Only — because a client with a mortgage already knows them from their bank.

"Sold in year" replaces a per-year Sell/Retain dropdown. The workbook has twenty of them per property, one for each year, and only one can ever be used. One field says the same thing and cannot contradict itself.

Where this differs from the spreadsheet

Three figures deliberately differ from Exposure.Retirement.Review (1).xlsx. They are on the screen because the adviser may have that spreadsheet open beside this page, and a difference nobody can account for is worse than no difference at all.

  • Tax is charged on today's rates. The workbook carries superseded thresholds. The average rate falls from 12.926% to 12.582%. 'Use of Assets in Retirement' AJ8:AJ11 and AL8:AL12 · ruled by Mike, 2026-09-13
  • The government pension is counted after tax across all twenty years. The workbook took it after tax on its summary sheet and before tax in its projection — a difference of $99.68 a week in year one. 'Asset & Cash Transactions' G26 · ruled by Mike, 2026-09-13
  • The sixth property earns from year one, like the other five. In the workbook its rows sat four years out, so it earned nothing for four years and, when sold, credited the client with nothing at all. 'Asset & Cash Transactions' rows 97–104 · ruled by Mike, 2026-09-13

The first two make the plan look worse and the third makes it look better. They are listed separately and never netted off against each other.

Step 4 — the decision that matters most

🔴 The verdict sentence. Drawn as "The plan holds — but it leans on selling three properties." Recommended because it is the honest reading of these numbers: the cash never hits zero, and the only reason it never hits zero is three property sales. A sentence that said only "The plan holds" would be true and misleading in the same breath.

The model itself draws no conclusion. The workbook prints the series and leaves the adviser to read them. Two readings are named in the code — whether the cash ever runs out, and how many years fall short — precisely so the screen is not inventing a judgement of its own. Everything else in the panel is arithmetic.

The banner says "Years spending more than they earn", not "Years in deficit". The second is what the code calls it; the first is what it means to somebody who does not work in finance.

What this screen does NOT do: it stores nothing, and it sends nothing to an AI. A household's income, pension, superannuation and six properties reach the calculation and are forgotten. There is no "Illustrative" badge on this report — these are the client's real numbers, and the badge would say the opposite.

What is NOT drawn here, and why

  • No file upload. Nothing produces a retirement position as an export, so every figure is typed. That is also why this is a Decision model and not a Report one.
  • No sliders. Four of the nine live screens have them; this one has ninety-odd inputs across three steps, and a slider per input would be unusable. Typed fields throughout, as on Lease vs Buy and the Loan Estimator.
  • No coach panel. The verdict panel and the differences card already say what an adviser would say. Adding a third block of prose would repeat them.
  • No second currency or country picker on the screen. Country comes from the firm, as it does everywhere else, and it is named under the tax table on step 2.
