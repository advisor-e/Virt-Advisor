Slice 1 collects three years of percentages. Step 4 shows one year. This page closes that gap, and it is the larger half of the job.

The arithmetic is already built and tested: computeThreeYearForecast chains all three years — each year’s closing balance sheet opens the next — and POST /api/report/three-way-forecast/three-years is live. No screen in the app has ever called either. Nothing on this page asks for new maths. It asks how three years should reach an advisor’s eyes, and a lender’s.

Every number below is the engine’s own output, run against one example client — the same client as the quick-fire drawing, with the same 8/6/5 % growth, 41/42/42 % margin and 4/3/3 % overheads you approved. The balance check reads zero in all thirty-six months. These are not figures drawn to look right.
“You should be able to choose 1, 2 or 3 year forecast please.” — Mike, answering question 1 below, and it replaced what was recommended to him (three years, always). It is the better answer: the advisor states how long a forecast they are building rather than the screen inferring it, and everything else on this page then follows from one control instead of from a rule nobody set.

The cost, found by reading the code rather than assuming. The engine hard-codes three — for (let y = 0; y < 3; y++), and its summary reads years[2]. Taking a year count is about four lines. What would NOT have been acceptable is computing three and showing fewer: the totals and the lowest-cash figure would then count years the advisor never asked for, and both would look perfectly reasonable on screen. The choice has to reach the engine.

State 2 — the same client with quick-fire OFF: “the same again”
An advisor who never ticks quick-fire has no way to make year 2 differ from year 1. So the obvious objection to showing three years always is that years 2 and 3 would be copies, and the screen would be two-thirds noise.

They are not copies, and this table is the engine proving it. Identical trading — the same 890,000 of revenue and the same 342,437 of gross profit in all three years — still gives three different profits, three different cash positions and three different balance sheets, because assets depreciate down and a term loan pays down. Profit rises 14,915 → 30,354 on flat sales. That is a real finding about the business, and today it is invisible.

Found while drawing this — not part of it
“Gross margin” means two different things on step 3 and step 4, and the two numbers disagree on screen.

On the quick-fire grid you type 41 % and the row says Gross margin. On step 4 the headline tile also says Gross margin and reads 39.0 % for the same year. Neither is wrong. The grid means margin on the goods — sell 100, the product in it cost 59. The headline means margin after everything charged above the gross line — the goods, plus freight, commissions and the two other direct costs, each a percentage of revenue.

🔴 SMALLER THAN IT FIRST LOOKED, and the correction is recorded rather than quietly dropped. This was first written up quoting a sixteen-point gap — 40.5 % against 24.5 %. That gap is real but it belongs to the engine’s built-in sample (the workbook company, which carries 10 % commissions and 3 % freight). The actual screen starts all four of those fields at zero (form.direct, ThreeWayForecastIntake.vue). An advisor who leaves them alone types 41 and sees 41. The gap opens only as far as they fill them in — knowingly, on the same step, a few inches from the grid. On the example client here it is 2 points, because freight is 2 %.

So: not a wrong figure, and not this job. It pre-dates quick-fire — the mark-up field and the headline have always meant different things. Written down because it was found here. If it is ever worth closing, the cheapest honest fix is one word on one screen — the step 4 tile reading Gross margin after direct costs — and that is a wording decision, so it is Mike’s. Nothing has been changed and nothing filed.

Mike ruled all seven one at a time, on his own standing instruction to be asked that way. The first ruling replaced the recommendation put to him — he chose a 1/2/3-year choice over the recommended “three years, always” — and the rest followed from it. Each ruling is recorded with the recommendation it answered, including the argument that was put against it, so a later session can see what was weighed rather than only what was chosen.

  1. ✅ RULED — the advisor chooses 1, 2 or 3 years. Mike, 2026-09-07: “good point — you should be able to choose 1, 2 or 3 year forecast please”. What was recommended and NOT chosen: three years always, no mode. The argument put for it was State 2 above — three “same again” years are not copies, and a rising profit on flat sales is a real finding. That argument survives his ruling intact; it is simply now a reason to offer three years rather than to impose them. The argument recorded against the recommendation was that an advisor who wanted one year would be handed two more they never typed — which is exactly what his answer removes.
  2. ✅ RULED — the choice lives on step 3, above the quick-fire tick. Mike, 2026-09-07, one word: “yes” to the recommendation. How long a forecast runs is an assumption about the forecast, and step 3 is where the assumptions are; it sits above quick-fire because it governs how many columns that grid shows. The argument recorded against it, because it is a real one: step 4 is where the years are actually looked at, so an advisor wanting a third year mid-review has to step back one screen. Step 4 was rejected because the control would sit among the tabs and read as a view toggle, while silently deciding the printed pack, the totals and the row saved for a client.
  3. ✅ RULED — the wording, as drawn. Mike, 2026-09-07: “yes”. The heading is How long a forecast?; the buttons are 1 year · 2 years · 3 years; the statement tabs are Year 1 · Year 2 · Year 3; each summary column carries its months beneath (Apr 25 – Mar 26); a year nobody typed into wears the amber same again tag. Short, and it matches the engine’s own language, so screen and code cannot drift into describing one thing two ways. The argument recorded against it: a client reading the printed pack may not know which calendar year “Year 2” is — which is what the months beneath each heading answer. Labelling the tabs by date outright was rejected: longer, and it breaks when a forecast starts mid-year. These five strings are now load-bearing — they are Mike’s, not ours, and they are not to be reworded by a later session.
  4. ✅ RULED — the printed pack carries every year that was asked for. Four pages for one year, seven for two, eleven for three. Mike, 2026-09-07: “yes”, on his own 2026-09-06 reasoning — a lender given one statement of three cannot check the claim that they tie, and the same holds one level up for one year of three. The argument recorded against it: eleven pages is a lot of paper for a client meeting, where four was enough. The middle option — the summary page plus year 1 in full, nothing for years 2 and 3 — was rejected because the advisor then cannot answer the first question a lender asks about year 3. A one-year forecast still prints exactly the four pages it prints today.
  5. ✅ RULED — the four headline tiles keep their labels and answer over the whole forecast. Mike, 2026-09-07: “yes”. The one that earns the change is Lowest point: the worst month of all thirty-six rather than of year 1. The engine already works it out — summary.lowestCash carries the value, the year, the month and the date — and no screen has ever shown it. On a one-year forecast all four read exactly as they do today. The argument recorded against it: “Gross margin” across three years is a blend of three different margins, a slightly softer number than today’s single-year one.
  6. ✅ RULED — it is one slice, not two. Mike, 2026-09-07: “yes”. The control, the engine’s year count, the screen, the cash line and the print land together. Splitting them would ship a three-year screen whose print still showed one year — the exact state that produced the 2026-09-06 ruling. The argument recorded against it: it is the larger half of 4.71 and it arrives in one go rather than in pieces he can look at as they come.
  7. ✅ RULED — a new forecast opens at one year. Mike, 2026-09-07: “one year default is fine”. Flagged to him as ours after the build, and ruled the next session. Three is one click away.
