Meeting Review

The open tab — whatever it happens to be. Nothing inside any tab changes. This item touches the menu and nothing else.

The tab you are reading has no dot. Opening a tab clears its dot at once and the count above drops by one. It does not wait for a save, a scroll or a reload.

Why five of seventeen tabs carry a dot in this drawing and not one or all. A drawing that shows one dot cannot show the rule for which colour wins, and one that dots everything looks like a fault rather than a signal. This is roughly what a real manager sees a fortnight in: two tabs they have never opened, two they have not been back to, and one with genuinely new material in it.

3 · When two apply at once

Red beats blue beats orange

A tab can qualify for two states at the same moment — never opened and holding new material, for instance. One dot shows, and it is the most urgent one.

  • Red first. Something arrived that this manager has not seen. That is the only state where somebody else has done something.
  • Then blue. Never opened at all is a bigger gap than not opened lately.
  • Then orange. The gentlest of the three — a nudge, not news.

⚠ This order is our judgement, not a ruling of yours, and it is flagged as one here rather than buried in the code. It matches the Handbook, where an unread page shows blue whatever its age. If you would rather a different order, this is the moment — it is one line to change now and a change to every screen later.

4 · The legend, and the count

The three colours mean nothing on their own the first time somebody sees them, so the foot of the menu says what they are — the same three lines the Handbook carries, in the same place, plus a count of how many tabs are asking for attention.

The count is the one piece here with no precedent in the app. The Handbook has it ("3 pages needing a look") and it earns its place for the same reason: a manager who has scrolled past the menu still knows whether anything wants them. It counts tabs, not items — a Compliance tab with four new documents in it counts once.

5 · What the app has to remember

One line per manager, per tab

Blue and orange are both questions about time — when did this person last open this tab? — so the app has to write that down. Red needs nothing stored, which is exactly why Compliance could ship its dot early.

  • It is the manager's own record, not the firm's. Two managers at one firm have their own dots and neither clears the other's.
  • One line per tab, never one line holding all seventeen. A manager with the hub open in two browser windows would otherwise overwrite themselves — the fault item 4.75 records, in miniature.
  • No new database table. It rides the storage every other per-person setting in this app already uses.
  • Nothing is stored about what is inside a tab — only that this person opened it, and when. No content, no counts, no client data.

Where a record has never been written, the tab is blue. That is the honest answer rather than a convenient one: on the day this ships, every tab every manager has ever used goes blue, because the app genuinely does not know they opened it. It clears itself the first time round the hub.

6 · Which hubs get this

All four — mentor, global group manager, group manager and firm manager. This is one menu component shared by every tier, so the dots arrive at all four the moment the component has them. There is no per-tier decision to make and none is being made quietly: a tier that does not show a tab simply has no dot for it.

7 · The judgements, stated

🔴 Red stays a per-tab signal, and only Compliance raises one. Item 4.84 describes red as "published since you last declared" — but declared is a Compliance word. No other tab in the hub knows what "new" would mean for itself, and inventing an answer for sixteen tabs would be building sixteen features nobody asked for. So the machinery is general and the signal is each tab's own. When a tab has real news to raise, it raises it, and the dot is already waiting.

⚠ Red beats blue beats orange — §3. Ours, not yours.

⚠ Three weeks is 21 days, matching the Handbook. Your words were "3 weeks", so this is arithmetic rather than a decision, but it is written down because a number in code with nothing behind it is how a rule quietly becomes a guess.
