Step 3 as it stands today

Every field on this step behaves exactly as it does now. The tick box adds one row at the top of the card and takes nothing away.

This is the whole of “untick it and the status quo continues”. Unticking does not clear anything and does not undo anything: the twelve monthly sales figures, the mark-up and the overheads are still whatever they were before the box was ticked. Quick-fire writes to its own set of figures and never over the top of yours.

Why it sits on step 3

Step 3 is where the forecast’s assumptions are set, and the forecast is built at step 4. A quick-fire entered anywhere later would be describing a forecast that had already been calculated.

Growth keeps last year’s shape

+8 % does not mean one twelfth a month. If the by-month P&L was dropped at step 1, each month is grown by the percentage against that same month last year — so a business with a quiet January and a heavy March keeps its January and its March. Cash flow, which is the whole point of a three-way, depends on it.

Where no by-month export was dropped, the year is spread evenly and the screen says so, because an even spread through a seasonal business produces a cash line that will not happen.

“Adds or subtracts from previous known data”

Year 1 grows on last year’s actuals, read from the file. Year 2 grows on Year 1. Year 3 grows on Year 2. Each year compounds on the one before it, which is what the three-year engine already does with the balance sheet: each year’s closing position opens the next.

This third screen is the real size of the job, and it is bigger than the grid. The maths for three years is already built and tested — computeThreeYearForecast chains all three, and POST /api/report/three-way-forecast/three-years is live. But no screen in the app has ever called it. Step 4 today draws one year: one profit and loss, one cash flow, one balance sheet, one set of charts.

So quick-fire is two pieces of work, not one: the grid you asked for, and a step 4 that can show three years. The grid is worth little without it — you would type three years of percentages and still only be shown the first.

Nothing on this page is open

Mike approved the drawing (“love it!”) and then ruled all five questions one at a time, on his own standing instruction to be asked that way. His last word was “looks good — move forward”, which is the authority to build. Each ruling is recorded with the recommendation it answered, including the argument that was put against it, so a later session can see what was weighed rather than only what was chosen.

  1. The row says “Gross margin”, with the mark-up beneath it. Recommended and chosen. Margin is the number an accountant says out loud and it is the word Mike used; the engine works in mark-up on cost and step 3’s existing field says so, therefore the conversion has to be visible or the two screens read as disagreeing. Rejected: labelling the row “Mark-up” to match the existing field and leaving the advisor to convert.
  2. Three rows and no more — sales growth, gross margin, overheads increase. Recommended and chosen. Nine boxes stay quick; eighteen do not, and at eighteen the full screen is the better tool. The argument recorded against it, because it is a real one: wages are usually the largest single overhead and often move differently from the rest, so one blended overheads percentage does lose something. A fourth row can be added later without redrawing — the grid simply grows.
  3. With no file dropped, the tick box appears but is greyed, reading “Needs last year’s Profit and Loss”. Recommended and chosen over hiding it. An advisor who cannot see the option assumes the app has none — which is the exact question Mike asked on 2026-09-07 that produced this drawing. Greyed with a reason says what to do next. Rejected: letting them type a base year by hand, which would mean typing last year’s figures in order to grow them — slower than the ordinary screen, so quick-fire would be making things worse.
  4. 🔴 Step 4’s three-year view is IN, and the work is TWO SLICES with the grid first. Recommended and chosen. The grid alone would collect three years of percentages and display one, so the three-year view is what gives it value; taking the grid first means the wording and the rows can still change cheaply before the larger half starts. The cost was stated before he ruled: the three-year view is a substantial screen — three years across a profit and loss, a cash flow and a balance sheet, plus the charts and the print — and it gets its own drawing for approval before it is built. This page does not approve it.
  5. All nine labels approved as drawn. Recommended and chosen. The one flagged as least certain was the grid heading “Add to, or subtract from, last year” — it describes a mechanism rather than naming a thing, which is unusual here, but it is the only line that tells an advisor the boxes take percentages rather than amounts and that a minus sign is allowed. Kept on that reasoning.
What this drawing does NOT change, stated so it is not assumed. The opening balance sheet, the loans, the asset schedule, the capital plans, the GST and tax settings, the debtor and creditor terms, the volatility read, the economic analysis at step 5, and every existing test of the single-year forecast. Quick-fire is an alternative way to fill in three fields; it is not an alternative forecast engine.
