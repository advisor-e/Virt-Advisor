What each model is for

The ten built models: what each one answers, the figures it puts on the screen, and the reading it gives you in plain English. Written so you can choose the right model before you open it — and know what it will not tell you.

Working Capital Cycle

How fast a fixed pot of cash recycles through stock and debtors, and what speeding that cycle up is worth in revenue and profit.

Key calculations

Working-capital cycle
days — cash tied up before you can trade again
Cycle factor
× per year
Annual revenue
from the capital employed
Net profit · monthly

Also on the screen

Working capital, monthly cash sales, total units, contribution margin, cash reserve required, return on working capital, the annual cycle factor, and the difference against a saved scenario in both dollars and per cent.

What the Coach tells you

Watch the wheel: your [working capital] of working capital flows Cash → Stock → Sale → Debtors and back. Right now that takes [n] days, so it turns about [n]× a month. Fixed costs sit outside the wheel — paid whether it turns or not.

If you cut it to [n − 10] days, the wheel would turn [n]× a month — about [amount] more revenue a year, at the same price.

Reach for it when

The client is profitable on paper but short of cash. Stock sits too long, customers pay slowly, or the owner asks where the money has gone. Also when they want to know what tightening terms is actually worth.

What you need

Initial investment and the share of it spent on plant and equipment; unit cost, mark-up and discount; days deliverable, days on hand, days receivable, days payable; monthly fixed costs.

What it does not cover

Illustrative teaching figures — no client data goes into it. It shows the mechanics of a cycle, not this client's cycle.

Debtor Business Drag

How much of a year's sales is not lost but merely delayed into next year because customers pay slowly — the drag — and how much two different collection profiles change it, month by month, including the deepest overdraft.

Key calculations

Deepest cash low — your plan
the worst month under the profile you set
Deepest cash low — Before
the same month on the frozen comparison
Effect of your decisions
better or worse at the worst month

Also on the screen

Both collection profiles in full (same month, one, two, three and four months, write-off), the drag difference between them, monthly stock paid, months in overdraft, and the year-end balance.

What the Coach tells you

Cash arrives late — customers pay across the months after the sale — but suppliers, wages and GST don't wait. Your bank dips to [amount] in [month], into overdraft, and you're in the red [n] months.

Collect earlier or pay suppliers slower and the blue line lifts; let collection drift later and you're forced to stretch suppliers, choking stock — the working capital cycle running backwards.

Reach for it when

Overdraft pressure, slow payers, or the question of whether to chase debtors harder or simply arrange a bigger overdraft. It puts a number on the choice.

What you need

Twelve months of sales, and two collection profiles: the share collected in the same month, at one, two, three and four months, and the share written off.

What it does not cover

Illustrative teaching figures — no client data goes into it. It models a collection profile, not this client's ledger.

EBITDA & Discounted Cash Flow

What the business is worth — normalised earnings before interest, tax and depreciation, and what its future cash is worth in today's money.

Key calculations

Enterprise Value
5-yr discounted cash flow + terminal
Normalised EBITDA (latest year)
after owner-pay normalisation
Discounted 5-yr cash flow
across the projected years
Terminal value
final year × exit multiple

Also on the screen

Actual growth year by year and the average of it, the projected EBITDA series, and the sum of the discounted years. An optional listed-company block compares an assessed share price against the market price.

What the Coach tells you

[n] years of accounts show normalised earnings moving from [from] to [to] — average actual growth [avg]. On your projected growth and discount rates, the business supports an enterprise value of [EV].

Note the [year] dip ([growth]) — growth is not a straight line, and a buyer will ask about that year.

The valuation is sensitive to the exit multiple: at [multiple] the terminal value is [share] of the total — test it either side before quoting a range.

And where the numbers don't support a price, it says so: “On these figures the valuation is not positive — the projected cash flows do not support a price. That is the honest read: the conversation is about restoring earnings before it can be about value.”

Reach for it when

A sale, a purchase, a succession plan, a shareholder buy-out, or simply the owner asking what the business is worth.

What you need

Several years of profit and loss, the normalisation add-backs, and the discount rate for each projected year.

What it does not cover

Two parts of the source workbook do not port and are not in this model: the interest calculations, and the margin-of-error and capitalised-earnings workings. Each projected year is discounted once at that year's rate rather than compounded — the source's own convention. It takes the client's real accounts, so it is never illustrative.

ProfitabilityTeaching aid

Margin · Mark-up · Break-even

The pricing trio every quote depends on: the difference between margin and mark-up, the sales needed to break even, and what a price change does to the volume required to hold the same profit. The whole model rests on one rule — required sales = (overheads + the owner's drawings) ÷ margin.

Key calculations

Margin
of the sale price
Mark-up
of the cost price
Cost of sales
of each sale dollar
Break-even · monthly
units to cover it

What the Coach tells you

Same deal, two numbers: your margin is [margin] (of the sale) but your mark-up is [mark-up] (of the cost) — don't confuse them when you quote.

To cover [overheads] overheads and take [drawings] in drawings, you must sell [amount] a month. Cut the price and that work climbs fast; raise it and it drops away. Price and drawings decide how hard the business has to run.

Reach for it when

Any pricing conversation. Discounting, quoting, a proposed price rise, or the classic question — if I drop the price ten per cent, how much more do I have to sell?

What you need

Selling price and unit cost; overheads; the owner's target drawings; and the price change to test.

What it does not cover

Illustrative teaching figures — no client data goes into it. It is a calculator for the arithmetic of pricing, not a costing system.

8 Levers Model

Which of the eight levers moves profit most, and which to pull first. The chain runs market size, foot traffic, prospects, customers, average spend, average frequency, revenue, margin, activity costs, fixed costs, profit — and the teaching point is that small nudges compound.

Key calculations

Revenue
Profit
Profit margin
Customers

Also on the screen

The full lever chain with each step's contribution, options B and C side by side against the current position, the profit increase in dollars and per cent, and a labour panel comparing charge-out rate against true cost per hour.

What the screen tells you

On the chain: “Every customer is won through this chain. Each step multiplies the last — which is why a small gain early compounds all the way to revenue.”

On the comparison: “Look at Option C: it earns LESS revenue than Option B, yet makes MORE profit — because the margin lever outweighs the volume lever. That is the whole lesson.”

On labour: “What the business charges per hour against what it actually pays per hour — once leave, sick days and non-chargeable time are taken out. The labour lines look far more profitable on paper than they are in practice.”

Reach for it when

Growth planning, and the question of where to start. It is strongest when an owner believes only one lever matters — usually more sales — and needs to see the others.

What you need

Either a trading position (a five-line product and labour mix, activity costs, fixed costs) or a set of funnel assumptions. It runs both ways.

What it does not cover

Every figure in it is illustrative teaching data, including the 880,000 trading income. It is never a description of a real business. One known oddity is reproduced from the source rather than repaired: on the Scenarios sheet the current column is anchored to trading revenue while options B and C are built forward from the funnel, so the sheet's own profit increase reads as a large negative. That is the workbook's, not a fault in the client's numbers, and it should not be explained to a client as a finding.

Cost of Capital (WACC)

What the money funding this business actually costs, blending what the owners expect on their equity with what the bank charges on debt. The result is the hurdle an investment has to clear to be worth doing.

Key calculations

Weighted average cost of capital
the hurdle an investment must clear
Cost of equity
what the owners' money expects to earn
Cost of debt (after tax)
the borrowing rate, less the tax saved
Funded by equity
and the balance funded by debt

Also on the screen

A beta helper deriving a growth rate, two candidate betas, average, standard deviation and volatility from a pasted index series — and a Test an investment panel that judges a specific investment against the hurdle.

What the screen tells you

Test an investment takes what the investment costs and what it should earn each year, and returns one of three verdicts: “Clears your cost of capital by [n] percentage points”, “Exactly meets your cost of capital”, or “Falls short of your cost of capital by [n] percentage points”.

It states its own strictness: “The bar is your cost of capital exactly as calculated above, with no safety margin added.”

It also warns when it cannot be trusted — too few periods to measure a spread, a beta outside the usual 0.3–2.5 range, or an index that does not move.

Reach for it when

Is this investment worth doing? Setting a hurdle rate, weighing a capital-structure change, or testing whether a project clears the cost of the money behind it.

What you need

Equity and debt amounts and rates, the tax rate, and a beta — or a pasted series of index values and shareholders' equity for the helper to work from.

What it does not cover

The beta helper offers a beta; the person using the model decides whether to take it. That relationship is deliberate and comes from the source workbook. It takes the client's real numbers, so it is never illustrative and the privacy boundary applies.

Lease vs Buy

Whether leasing or buying an asset — a vehicle in the source model — costs less over its whole life, and which of the two to recommend.

What the screen tells you

The model does not leave the choice open. It returns a one-word verdict — “Lease!” or “Buy!” — with the line: “That's the cheaper option — [amount] less over the term.”

Reach for it when

Funding a vehicle or a piece of equipment, and the client is weighing a lease against a loan.

What you need

Asset price; loan amount, rate, term and amortisation method; depreciation method and rate; running costs; the lease term, payments and lease-end costs; and the tax rate.

What it does not cover

The workbook's separate FBT-versus-reimbursement sheet is not part of the verdict and is not built — if the question is really about fringe benefit tax, this model does not answer it. It takes the client's real numbers, so it is never illustrative.

The Loan Estimator

What lenders would lend against the security the client owns, whether the household can service it, and what the repayments would be.

Key calculations

Maximum business loan
against the business, on its EBIT — an indication only
Monthly surplus
the number the affordability test is judged on
Available security
across all assets, after existing debt
Monthly repayment
on the proposed loan from the quick calculator
Estimated maximum borrowing
with everything else as entered — an indication only

Also on the screen

Current equity, the stressed deposit required and the stress payment gap, debt-to-value / equity-to-value / loan-limit-to-value ratios, a household serviceability breakdown with the tax bands used, and a ten-year overdraft interest and closing-balance table.

What the screen tells you

It reaches a verdict on affordability — “Meets the affordability test” or “Falls short of the affordability test” — immediately qualified: “An indication of affordability only — not a lending decision.”

The serviceability panel shows its working: total net monthly income, minimum monthly loan payments, and the monthly expenses used — “the greater of actual expenses and the bank's minimum allowances”.

Reach for it when

Borrowing capacity, refinancing, or a client asking whether the bank will lend for a purchase and whether they can afford it.

What you need

Each security item the client owns — its class, market value, current debt, growth prospects and any value adjustment — plus household income and existing commitments.

What it does not cover

The stress-tested payment is the bank's worst-case figure — the full lending limit at the assessment rate over the maximum term — not the client's actual loan terms, and it should never be described as their repayment. The lending rules themselves come from a configuration file rather than from any one bank. It takes the client's real numbers, so it is never illustrative.

Multiple Property Assessment

Whether a rental property is worth buying, and whether a household's portfolio of up to five works — ten years of cash flow, tax with ring-fenced losses carried forward, both loans amortised, and the equity position.

Key calculations

Weekly cash position
year 1 — one property, or all of them
Total debt
year 1
Net equity
year 10
Return on investor funds
year 10

Also on the screen

A ten-year investment summary: assumed property value, total debt position, net equity, cash deposit, annual cash top-up, capital introduced, cumulative investor funds, and the weekly cash profit or loss. Plus the loan apportionment across properties and the household's consolidated servicing.

What the Coach tells you

Per property: “This property costs the client [amount] a week for the first [n] years… It turns cash-positive in year [n]… Over ten years it builds [equity] of equity against [funds] of money put in — a return of [rate].” Where it never turns, it says so.

On tax: either “No tax is payable across the ten years” or “Tax first becomes payable in year [n]” — and whether losses are ring-fenced against the property's own future income or offset against the client's other income.

Across the portfolio: whether the deposit stretches to every property, the combined loan-to-value against the ceiling, the ten-year cash demand on top of the deposit — closing on the question it is built to ask: “The question is not whether the portfolio grows. It is whether the family can carry [amount] a week in the hardest year while it does.”

Reach for it when

A client is buying a rental, or already owns several and wants to know whether the portfolio holds together over ten years rather than in the first month.

What you need

Per property: purchase price, rent, operating costs and loan structure. For the portfolio: the family home and the loan apportionment across the properties.

What it does not cover

Its tax treatment follows the firm's own Property Tax Rules settings — what may be depreciated, whether rental losses ring-fence, interest deductibility — which a group or firm manager sets. It is therefore the firm's stated position, not universal tax advice, and it is not a substitute for a tax opinion. It takes the client's real numbers, so it is never illustrative.

Quick Position

A fast read on where the business stands right now — how much cash it can actually reach, how long that lasts if sales stop, and what it must sell to break even.

Key calculations

Quick cash available
realisable now, after immediate debts
Cover at zero sales
months on the business's own resources
With life-line capital
months, adding outside support
Break-even sales
needed per month to stand still

What the Coach tells you

“If sales stopped today, the business could cover its outgoings for [n] months from its own quick cash — [n] months with the life-line capital. To stand still it needs [amount] of sales a month at the current margin.”

The discount trap: “a [n]% discount means selling [n]% more just to keep the same gross profit.”

Where the position is already negative it changes tone entirely: “Immediate debts exceed what the business can realise quickly. This is a now conversation: which creditors can wait, what can be turned to cash this week, and what outside support exists.”

Reach for it when

Distress, a sudden stop in trading, a lost major customer, or the owner asking how long they have. It is the model for the first conversation, not the detailed one.

What you need

Current cash, debtors, stock and creditors; monthly fixed costs; gross profit percentage; and any life-line capital the owner could call on.

What it does not cover

A service business with no stock removes the stock line from the model entirely rather than showing a misleading zero — set that, or the reading is wrong. Where a figure would divide by zero it returns nothing rather than a fabricated zero, so an unlimited runway shows as unlimited. The New Zealand COVID wage-subsidy block of the source workbook does not port. It takes the client's real numbers, so it is never illustrative.
