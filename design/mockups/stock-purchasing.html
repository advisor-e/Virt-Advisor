Decision 5 — RULED BY MIKE 2026-09-13 — the bands have gaps, and a line that lands in one scores nothing

✓ Ruled as recommended. Every gap is closed — each ladder runs continuously from the bottom of its lowest rung to the top of its highest. A DEVIATION FROM THE WORKBOOK: Widget 3's unit cost risk goes from 0 to 5, and this gets pinned in the golden test against the workbook's own cached figure, with both numbers recorded.

The evidence. Every ladder is a list of closed ranges, and the ranges do not meet. On share of stock held, Trickle ends at 0.125 and Flowing starts at 0.135 — a line at 0.13 belongs to neither. Every one of the five has gaps; they only stay hidden on the two criteria that happen to be counted in whole numbers.

🔴 And it bites in the workbook's own first three rows. Widget 3's average unit cost is $25.22, which falls in the $25–26 gap. It scores 0 for unit cost risk — a cheap, low-risk item, marked as though the criterion did not exist.

Recommended: close every gap, so each ladder runs continuously from the bottom of the lowest rung to the top of the highest. Widget 3 would then score 5. This changes no score that is currently right, and it is the smallest change that makes the ladder mean what it appears to mean. The alternative — port the gaps faithfully — reproduces a fault we would then have to explain to every advisor who hit it.

Decision 6 — RULED BY MIKE 2026-09-13 — what happens in a gap, which the workbook answers two different ways

✓ Ruled as recommended. One rule on both sheets: a criterion with no band scores 0. A DEVIATION FROM THE WORKBOOK on the Product Ratings side — Widget 9's total goes from 9.13 to 9 — pinned in the golden test against the workbook's own cached figure, with both numbers recorded. The Sales Report side already scores 0 and is unchanged. The wrong-cell reference in the "how many sold" formula is fixed to use the quantity, as its four siblings do.

🔴 Both are visible in the sample. Widget 3 scores 0 for unit cost risk (above). And on the other sheet Widget 9 scores 9.13 out of 25 — its share of stock is 0.13, lands in the 0.125–0.135 gap, and 0.13 is added to its total as 0.13 of a point. Four criteria gave it 9; the fifth gave it a fraction of a percent.

The second is the reason a score can arrive with decimal places on a screen where every rung is a whole number out of 25.

Recommended: one rule, on both — a criterion with no band scores 0. Once Decision 5 closes the gaps this can only happen on a blank or a negative, where 0 is the honest answer. The two decisions work together: 5 stops lines falling through, 6 makes the fall harmless when it does.

One more of the same family, found while checking the above and not worth its own decision. The “how many sold” formula on Sales Report tests the wrong cell in its middle branch — I7, the entry date, where every sibling uses D7, the quantity. It is harmless while an entry date is present, and a line of 11–15 units without one scores 0 instead of 3. The fix is to use the quantity, as the other four branches do.

Decision 7 — RULED BY MIKE 2026-09-13 — how much of steps 2, 3 and 4 gets built

✓ Ruled as recommended. All four steps get built, as drawn. Step 1 is a port of the workbook; steps 2 and 3 are new work — five inputs and the quick ratio between them — asked for here and approved here, so they are not an inference from anything.

The whole of the spreadsheet is step 1. Its Process sheet describes steps 2, 3 and 4 in nine sentences and provides not one cell for them. So the ranking is a port; everything drawn above under “the shelf, and the bank” is a proposal.

Recommended: build all four steps, as drawn. Steps 2 and 3 are five inputs and one ratio between them — perhaps thirty lines of maths — and without them the model answers “what is worth buying” while saying nothing about whether the client can pay for it. The workbook's own instruction is “do not approve purchases that compromise short-term liquidity”, so a port that stops at the ranking stops one step before the point.

The honest alternative is to build step 1 alone and leave the ranking as the whole model. It is less work and it is defensible — but it ships the half that is fun without the half that is prudent.

One thing to settle at the same time as Decision 5 and 6: those two rulings are deviations from the workbook, so each one gets pinned in the golden test against the workbook's own cached figure, the way the Mid-Level Budget's three were. The test records both numbers and says which one we chose and why.

Two things in the workbook that need no decision, recorded so they are not re-found. Its margin column divides gross profit by sales with no guard, so a line that sold nothing gives #DIV/0! — 33 of them sit in the workbook today, all in blank rows, but a real client can have a zero-sales line and ours must score it rather than break. And the file carries 3,190 #REF! cells, every one below row 1850 in the unused template region, none in a live calculation. Neither is a fault in the method; both are things a faithful port would otherwise inherit.
