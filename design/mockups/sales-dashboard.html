Decision 9 — RULED BY MIKE 2026-09-13 — add dates, and what that costs

✓ Ruled yes, in his own words on being told the workbook has no dates: “yes but good idea, can we add dates”. This is a deliberate extension BEYOND the workbook — the first thing on this page that the spreadsheet does not contain in some form — and it is recorded as his request rather than inferred from the gap.

Most of it is already built. server/report/intake/salesSheetReader.js, written for Stock Purchasing earlier the same day, already reads a Sale Date column and already handles the three ways a date arrives: an Excel serial, a real date cell, and a text date. It refuses to guess — "03/04/2021" is 3 April or 4 March depending where you live, so the ambiguous form is rejected rather than silently resolved, and a stray number below a sane threshold never becomes 4 January 1900.

⚠ The one real cost, named rather than discovered mid-build. That reader demands both Sale Date and Entry Date, because Stock Purchasing needs the gap between them for days-on-hand. This screen needs only the sale date. Shared unchanged, the reader would reject a perfectly good file for missing a column this model never uses. The required-columns list has to become per-model rather than one fixed list.

Built as: one trend card, fed by a Sale Date column, shown only when the file really has one. A firm whose export carries no dates sees the screen exactly as it is without this card — not an empty chart, and never a fabricated date. Reuses the existing LineChart component.

Decision 2 — the bands are the owner's to set, not ours to fix

The nine ceilings above (250 · 500 · 1,000 · 1,500 · 2,500 · 5,000 · 7,500 · 10,000 · 15,000) are typed cells in the workbook, not constants buried in a formula. A jeweller and a dairy do not share a set of sales bands, and a $250 top band is meaningless to one and the whole business to the other.

This is the correction Mike made on Stock Purchasing earlier today — “the whole point of the model is to allow a business owner to quantify their expectations — therefore, all the rankings need to be variables” — applied here from the start rather than after the fact.

Recommended: the nine ceilings are typed on screen and recalculate the table, and a ceiling that crosses its neighbour pushes the neighbour aside rather than being refused — the same behaviour built for Stock Purchasing's ladders, so the two models behave alike.

Decision 3 — DEVIATION · the workbook counts a sale into one band and banks its money in another

The band totals and the band counts do not use the same test. For the $1,501–$2,500 band the money is summed with SUMIFS(… ">="&1501, … "<="&2500) but the transactions are counted with COUNTIFS(… ">="&1501, … "<"&2500). The next band up is worse: it sums from ">="&2501 but counts from ">"&2501.

What that does to a real client. A sale of exactly $2,500 adds its value and its margin to the band but is counted in no band at all. So does a sale of exactly $2,501, and so does one of exactly $5,000. The transactions column then disagrees with the money beside it, and the Total line disagrees with the sum of the rows — with nothing on screen to say why.

It is invisible in the sample data, which happens to contain no sale on any of those three figures, so all ten bands add to 140 and the sheet looks perfectly healthy. Round numbers like $2,500 and $5,000 are exactly the amounts real invoices land on.

Recommended: every band is “at least the floor, at most the ceiling” for the count and the money alike, so a sale falls in exactly one band and the columns always reconcile. Pinned in the golden test with the workbook's own figure recorded beside ours.

Decision 4 — DEVIATION · “Total # of Sales” reads a shorter list than everything else

The headline sales count comes from COUNTIF(E18:E508,">=1"). Every other formula on the sheet reads further down the same column — the band sums reach row 535, the dimension totals reach 529, the revenue total reaches 518. Four different end points for one list.

What that does to a real client. Nothing at 140 rows. At roughly 491 sales the count quietly stops rising while the money keeps going, so the average sale value — which is the money divided by that count — starts climbing for no reason. It is a wrong number that looks entirely plausible, which is the kind UAT cannot catch.

Recommended: one list, read once, however long it is, with the count, the value and the margin all taken from the same rows. Pinned in the golden test.

Decision 5 — twenty charts become one chart with two controls

The workbook carries 25 charts across two sheets — and they are 20 unique views drawn twice over: five dimensions (brand, product, category, region, salesperson) times three measures (transactions, value, margin) as doughnuts, plus one bar chart per dimension. The two sheets are literally named “Pie Graph Options” and “Other Chart Options”: they are a menu, laid out as a wall of charts, because a spreadsheet has no other way to offer a choice.

A web page does. The card above is one chart and one table with a row of dimensions and a row of measures — every one of the twenty views, one click apart, and the ranked table beside it that a doughnut alone cannot give you. It also drops the 3-D bars, which distort the very comparison they are drawn to make.

Recommended: one card, five dimensions, three measures — as drawn. Reusing the existing DoughnutChart and HBarChart components, so no new charting library and no bundle growth.

Decision 6 — the salesperson cut ranks named employees, and that is a decision for you

One of the five dimensions is Sales Person, by name — Billy, Bobby, Betty and the rest. Choosing it turns this screen into a staff performance ranking: who sold the most, who earned the least margin, ordered best to worst. Every other cut ranks a product or a place.

This is the client's own data about their own staff, held in their own spreadsheet today, and an advisor talking to an owner about where the margin goes will reasonably want it. It is also the kind of data that, on a screen rather than in a private workbook, gets shared further than intended.

Recommended: keep the cut, with three limits stated in the build rather than assumed — it is never sent to the model, it never leaves the firm, and it is the only cut whose column may be left out of the import without the screen losing anything else. Marked on the tab above with a small amber dot, so nobody opens it by accident in front of a room.

Say no and the tab simply goes; the other four cuts are untouched and nothing else on the screen changes.

Decision 7 — DEVIATION · the last salesperson's transaction count points at the wrong person

On the workbook's calculation sheet, Shaun's transaction count is =Z9 — which is Sue's count. It should be =AA9. Every one of the other nineteen cells in that block points where it should.

It is invisible in the sample data, because the lockstep sample gives all ten salespeople exactly 14 sales each, so Sue's number and Shaun's number are the same number. On real data the last name in the list would silently show someone else's sales count beside its own money — and it would still add up to the right total, because the total is calculated separately.

Recommended: count each name from its own column. There is nothing to weigh up here; it is a typo in a spreadsheet. Recorded as a decision only so that the golden test says why our figure and the workbook's would differ on a real file.

Decision 8 — where the data comes from, and what happens when a column is missing

The workbook is typed in by hand: someone keys 140 rows onto the Sales Data Input sheet. We already have the other half of this — server/report/intake/salesSheetReader.js, built for Stock Purchasing earlier today, which reads a sales report and already takes product, revenue, cost and margin.

What it does not yet read is the four columns that make the cuts: brand, product category, region and salesperson. And a real firm will be missing some — plenty of businesses have no regions and no brands.

⚠ Decision 9 adds a fifth optional column and one real change to that reader. Its required-columns list is a single fixed set demanding Entry Date, which this model never uses; it has to become per-model, or a good file gets rejected for a missing column nothing here reads.

Recommended: reuse that reader, and let each cut appear only if its column is really in the file — a firm with no region column simply does not see a Region tab, rather than seeing an empty one or a fabricated one, and a file with no Sale Date simply has no trend card. The bands table and the headline figures need only revenue and cost, so the screen always works even on the barest file.

The alternative — typing it in, as the workbook does — stays available for a firm without an export, exactly as the Mid-Level Budget is typed.
