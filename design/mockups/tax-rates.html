Item 4.81 — Tax Rates per country. Drawn for approval 2026-09-09. Nothing is built.

What it fixes. The Three-Way Forecast applies a 28% company tax rate and a 15% GST rate to every client in every country, on a New Zealand filing cycle. An advisor can type over the two rates — but nothing on the screen says the numbers in front of them are New Zealand's, no manager can set what a country's figures should be, and no figure names a source. A forecast for an Australian client is wrong on tax whether or not the client owns a single asset, and it balances perfectly.

Why a separate tab. Mike renamed Depreciation Rates on 2026-09-09 precisely because a tab called Tax Rules promised GST and company tax and delivered a depreciation schedule. These two share their machinery in the code — one country table, one four-tier cascade, one approve-before-use gate — and nothing on screen.

It rides what item 4.78 already built. ISO country codes, the cascade through scopeChain, a store that structurally cannot hold an unapproved table, a mandatory source document and page on every figure, newer-document-wins with the older figure shown beside it, and per-row origin badges. This drawing adds no new machinery — only a second kind of figure travelling through it.

1 · Where the tab sits

Beside Depreciation Rates, at the end of the same group, on all four manager tiers — mentor, global group manager, group manager, firm manager. Appending moves nothing already on a manager's screen.

All four tiers, not mentor-alone. The default since 2026-08-24 is the mentor tier by itself, and the judgement has to be stated rather than assumed. It is stated here: a tax rate is national, a firm advises clients in more than one country, and a firm in Australia must be able to approve Australian figures without waiting for the mentor to do it for them. This is the same reasoning that put Depreciation Rates on four tiers, and it is the same table underneath.

2 · The screen — one country at a time

Same shape as the Depreciation Rates tab, deliberately. A country selector, the figures this level works to, where each came from, and the documents this level holds. A manager who has used one screen has used both.

Considered and rejected: one long table with every country in it. It reads as a comparison, and nobody compares tax regimes — they set one country's figures and move on. It would also make the approval question ambiguous: approving the table would mean approving countries the manager never looked at.

3 · The four figures, each with its source

Four figures, not two. The item was filed as "the tax rate and the GST rate", and that turned out to be incomplete: the forecast's GST section also fixes how often a return is filed and whether it is filed on invoices or on cash. Both are New Zealand's today, and the filing cycle changes when the client pays, not just what they pay. A right rate on a wrong filing cycle is still a wrong cash flow.

Every figure carries its document, page and publication date — the same rule as a depreciation rate, for the same reason: an unsourced number in an approved table is indistinguishable from a sourced one on the page a lender reads.

5 · The filing cycle is the part that is not just a number
This is the real work in item 4.81, and it is in the engine, not on this screen The forecast does not hold a general filing cycle. It holds three named branches — one-monthly, two-monthly and six-monthly — written straight into server/report/threeWayForecastModel.js. Those are New Zealand's three cycles. Australia's quarterly BAS cannot be expressed at all, and neither can the UK's quarterly VAT. Making the cycle country-aware means replacing three hardcoded branches with one that takes a number of months.

Why this is drawn rather than left to the build. Everything else in this item is a screen and a stored figure. This one changes a calculation that already has a known fault in it — the six-monthly branch was the workbook's own #REF! in the first month of each year, and is reproduced deliberately (correction R5). Anyone rewriting those branches has to keep that behaviour for New Zealand while adding a cycle New Zealand does not have.

Recommended: the stored figure is a number of months (1, 2, 3, 6, 12) with the country's own name for it beside it — Quarterly, Two-monthly, BAS. The engine reads the number; the manager and the advisor read the name. One generic branch replaces three, and a country with a cycle nobody anticipated needs no code.

6 · One number may not be enough for a company tax rate
Flagged, not solved — and deliberately not drawn as solved Australia has two company rates: a lower one for a base rate entity and the standard one for everything else, with a turnover and passive-income test between them. New Zealand has one flat company rate and a different flat rate for trusts. A single "company tax rate" box cannot express that, and this drawing does not pretend otherwise: it shows one editable figure, sourced, which a manager sets to whichever rate their clients actually pay.

Why one box is still the right answer for now. The forecast applies a single flat rate to monthly profit — taxOnMonthProfit[m] = netSurplusBeforeTax[m] × taxRate. It has no concept of bands, entity types or eligibility tests, and giving it one is a different and much larger piece of work than this item. The honest position is: one sourced, manager-approved figure is a large improvement on one unsourced hardcoded figure, and pretending to model eligibility would be worse than either.

Recommended: the figure carries a free-text "which entities this applies to" line, in the manager's own words, shown to the advisor beside the rate. A firm writes "base rate entities — turnover under $50m" and the advisor reading the forecast can see whether their client is one. It is a sentence, not a rule engine, and it costs nothing.

Mike's own words, 2026-09-09: "overall profit tax rates in bands for individuals with flat rates for companies and trusts etc". Bands for individuals are out of scope and stated as such — the Three-Way Forecast models a trading entity, not a person.

8 · What this drawing deliberately does NOT do
  • It does not model tax bands, entity types or eligibility tests. One flat rate on monthly profit, which is what the engine has. §6 says why, and says what is offered instead — a sentence in the manager's own words, not a rule engine.
  • It does not touch provisional or instalment tax. The forecast pays tax on a schedule it already has. When that schedule falls due is a separate question from what rate applies, and nobody has asked for it.
  • It does not touch the Multiple Property Assessment's tax settings. That is a separate block on the Property Tax Rules tab, ruled on differently, and it stays where it is.
  • It does not assert any country's tax figures. Every figure on every screen above came from a document a firm loaded. The app ships no tax rates and never will — the same position Depreciation Rates takes, for the same reason.
  • It does not add a class-match step. §4 says why: there is nothing to match.
  • It does not build the country field. Already approved, still unbuilt, shared with item 4.78.
9 · What has to be decided before anything is built
  • The filing cycle becomes a number of months with a country's own name beside it (§5). This replaces three hardcoded branches in the forecast engine with one, and it is the only change in this item that touches a calculation.
  • The company tax rate carries a free-text "which entities this applies to" line (§6), shown to the advisor beside the rate.
  • Four figures, not two (§3) — the filing cycle and the accounting basis are in scope, because a right rate on a wrong cycle is still a wrong cash flow.
