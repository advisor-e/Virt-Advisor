The working, so it can be checked. September's container costs 90,000. The deposit is 60% — 54,000 — plus the 10% exchange allowance, so 59,400 leaves in May, four months ahead. In September freight is 12% of 90,000 (10,800) and duty is 5% (4,500). Border GST is 15% of the landed value, which is the exchange-adjusted stock cost (99,000) plus freight and duty: 114,300 × 15% = 17,145. The 40% balance, 36,000 plus the allowance, is 39,600 in October. January's container of 60,000 runs the same way, its deposit falling in September. 131,445 has left the business by the end of September against stock that cost 90,000 and has not yet been sold.

WHY this is the section a bank reads first

Today all of this would appear as one line called paid to creditors, in the month the supplier was settled. The deposit four months earlier, the freight, the duty and the GST at the border would all be invisible — and they are the reason the business needs the money.

Two of these five rows come back: the border GST is reclaimed on the next return, and the stock eventually sells. A funding request is about the months in between, and that is exactly what these rows draw.

What changes in the GST return

Three corrections, all of them cash

1. Overseas sales stop being charged GST. Today every sale is charged, so an exporter's return is overstated every filing period. With the tick set, no GST arises on them at all.

2. GST on imported stock moves to the border. Today it would fall when the supplier is paid. It really falls when the goods clear customs, and it is charged on the landed value — stock plus freight plus duty — not on the supplier's invoice alone.

3. It is still claimed back. Border GST is an input credit on the same return as everything else, so the forecast shows the payment and the credit in their real months rather than netting them into one figure.

There is also one piece of plumbing with no decision in it: on the Cash GST basis the return is currently worked backwards from total money received. Once some receipts are zero-rated that sum is wrong, so the two streams' collections have to be counted separately.

The guard that gets written first
Before any of this is built, one test is written and seen to pass: with the tick off and both overseas series empty, the forecast must produce byte-identical output across all 10,155 cells of the golden set. It is the only thing that proves Mike's workbook port is undisturbed — and this change reaches into the GST computation, which the golden set covers cell by cell.
Open — four questions for Mike

Not decided, and not to be decided by a developer

  1. Every label on this drawing is a proposal. “Stock arriving from overseas”, “Deposits paid overseas”, “Duty and clearance”, “Exchange-rate movement”, “This business buys or sells overseas” — all mine, none yours. Correct them on this page and they become the built wording.
  2. A deposit can fall before the forecast starts. Stock landing in June with a four-month lead was paid for in February — two months before a forecast opening 1 April. That cash is already gone and sits in the opening balance sheet. Should the screen (a) simply not show a deposit outside the window, (b) warn that one falls outside it, or (c) ask for it as an opening prepayment? Found while drawing this; it is not in the request and has no obviously right answer.
  3. Does the exchange allowance apply to overseas SALES too? The drawing applies it to purchases only. Applied to sales it would reduce what is banked, with the shortfall joining the same direct-cost line. Symmetrical and arguably more honest — but it is a second place a single percentage moves the numbers, so it is your call.
  4. Slice 2's two rulings, which do not block approval of this drawing but do block that half being built: the 30-day-to-calendar-month conversion, and whether the workbook's real unit costs or the forecast's mark-up wins where they disagree. Both change the numbers. Neither is a developer's call.
