Why this list exists

The old list recorded who each item was waiting for and never who wanted it. Once anything was written down, an AI’s passing observation and Mike’s instruction looked identical — so every later session treated both as work. On 15 August two pieces of unrequested work reached him dressed as priorities. One had grown from a single AI-written sentence; the other from a cell in a mockup that didn’t match the code.

“Unless I specifically asked for it, unless it meets all my criteria for building a better app, I want it deleted — off the list, not parked.”

An audit that day cut thirty-one items to fourteen. One had already been built seventeen days earlier. Three were the same blocker written three times. Two existed only to maintain a document nobody read. A whole section of “tidying” broke the list’s own rule that a warning is not a task.

How an item is scored

5 Security, privacy or data integrity Data could leak, reach the wrong tier, or be lost without anyone noticing
4 The user is worse off without it An adviser or their client gets worse advice, a wrong screen, or no screen
3 It helps sell the package A competitive advantage, or something a buyer asks to see before committing
2 Robustness — it fails better Invisible until something breaks, and then it decides how bad the break is
1 Internal only Helps us work. No customer would ever see it
0 Fails all of it Serves no user, fixes nothing, sells nothing — deleted, with its code

“Asked by” is the field that matters most. Not who the item waits on — who wanted it. Neither of the two wasted jobs could have filled it in. Anything marked ours has no requester outside this project and has to justify itself or go.

The five fields are enforced, not encouraged. A guard test fails the build if any item in the repository is missing a score, a why, a risk, an asked by or a touches. That is what stops this decaying back into a list of notes.
