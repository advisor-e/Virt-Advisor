The three corrections to the spreadsheet

Ruled by Mike, 2026-09-13 — why some figures here differ from the workbook

1 · The “4 months later” collection slot only ever worked once. Every other timing row in the spreadsheet locks its percentage in place; that one does not, so it collects in its first month and is ignored for the rest of the year. On this sample a client collecting 10% four months later should see $21,250 across the year; the spreadsheet finds $2,500. The other $18,750 is 5.4% of the year's revenue — and the “balance” check still reads 100% throughout, so nothing warns them.

2 · GST was counted twice in the bank balance. Your ruling of 2026-09-12 on the High Level Budget, and these are the same rows with the same formulas. The closing balance here is −$54,040 where the spreadsheet shows −$39,697; the difference is exactly the GST held for Inland Revenue, which was being treated as the business's own cash.

3 · Income that carries no GST was being taxed. The spreadsheet's GST base swept in tax rebates, interest received and capital introduced. None of the three bears GST — capital introduced is not even a sale. This moves no figure on this sample, where all three are empty.

Everything else is the spreadsheet, line for line. Each correction is named in the model's header and pinned in the golden test against the workbook's own figure, so any of them can be checked by hand.

What the sample shows, and why it is worth seeing

This client invoices $348,300 and is profitable on paper — yet the budget runs the bank down to −$54,040 by March. Nothing is wrong with the business; the money simply arrives later than the wages go out. That is the entire argument for this model over the High Level Budget, and it is visible on the first screen.
