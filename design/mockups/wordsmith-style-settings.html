How Wordsmith uses this material

Five steps, run once for each Alignment Statements recording. The AI does three of them; the app's own code does the other two and checks the AI's work. "Reads" names the material on this page that each step uses.

  1. Sort what was saidAIthen code
    The AI reads the transcript and points, by line number, at what the client said about each of the five statements, sorting by meaning. The code then takes those lines word for word. Anything the advisor said is dropped, and a statement nobody spoke about stays empty.
    Reads: each statement's name and its first Alignment definition row.
  2. Find what is missingCode
    The code checks each statement against its questions for the room: is there a date for the Vision, a date and a measure for the Mission? Anything missing becomes a question for the advisor and client, never an invention. The Strategy's scope is judged by the AI in step 4.
    Reads: the questions for the room.
  3. Turn the style into settingsAI
    The room's own words about what the statements are for and how they should sound become four settings the advisor can see: sentence length, formality, jargon and voice, plus a tone and a reader.
    Reads: the list of style choices.
  4. Draft each statementAI
    One statement at a time, from the client's own sorted words only. The AI is told what the statement is, with the Alignment document first and winning wherever best practice differs. It is also told how to write for the chosen style, and the rules: nothing the client did not say, their strongest phrases kept, New Zealand spelling, and the word limit.
    Reads: every definition row, the style instruction for each chosen setting, the word limit, and once saved, the statement's domain writing rule.
  5. Check every draftCode
    The code checks each draft for numbers and names nobody said, American spelling, and length. A failed draft is written once more with the failures listed; anything still wrong is shown to the advisor, never hidden.
    Reads: the word limit.

Never sent to the AI: the sources behind each best-practice row, and last night's notes in the amber panels below. They are there for the people shaping the content.

Where the Knowing, Relating, Having and Doing rules come from

Background you supplied on 2026-09-29: the five Meta-Content Domains of NLP and Neuro-Semantics (L. Michael Hall), the "filing cabinets" for where a person directs attention and language.

  • Being: identity and core state; the top of Dilts' Logical Levels. "Who am I?"
  • Knowing: beliefs and how we make sense of reality; the Meta-Model and Meta-Programs. "What do I believe?"
  • Relating: connection and how we treat others; rapport and perceptual positions (self, other, observer). "How do I treat others, and how do they treat me?"
  • Having: getting and keeping resources; Well-Formed Outcomes and Toward/Away-From motivation. "How do I get what I need, and hold onto it?"
  • Doing: action, behaviour and strategy; the TOTE model. "What steps do I take?"

Your Alignment deck's sheet 6 already writes Values this way: "Who we're talking to / What we say / What we mean".

Your Alignment document says

Speaking to your stakeholders: where you see the business in future, and when. It answers Where and When, and delivers Contextual Awareness.

Best practice says

  • A picture of a future the business can actually reach, with a finish line, so everyone will know when it has been achieved (Collins & Porras).
  • Pitched as a stretch: a 10 to 30 year goal with roughly 50 to 70 per cent odds, which the team still believes it can hit.
  • Described vividly enough that people can picture it. It turns the vision from words into pictures.
  • Specific to this business. "Recognised leader in the insurance field" fails because any firm could say it (Collis & Rukstad).

How it differs from its neighbours

Vision can be reached and carries a date. Purpose is never finished. In your method, Mission is the next milestone on the way to the Vision, and Strategy is how you intend to get there.

Sources

Collins & Porras, Building Your Company's Vision, HBR 1996 Collis & Rukstad, Can You Say What Your Strategy Is?, HBR 2008

Your Alignment document says

Speaking to both Customers and Stakeholders: why you do what you do beyond economic requirement. It answers Why, and delivers Frameworks.

Best practice says

  • The reason the business exists beyond making money. It lasts indefinitely and is never achieved: "a guiding star on the horizon, forever pursued but never reached" (Collins & Porras).
  • Found by asking "why is that important?" five times, starting from what the business makes.
  • A common form is "To [contribution] so that [impact]" (Simon Sinek).
  • It does not have to be unique, and "maximise shareholder wealth" is never a purpose.

How it differs from its neighbours

Vision says where you are going; Purpose says why. Values guide how you behave on the way. Drucker, and Collis & Rukstad, call this statement the "mission". In your method it is Purpose.

Where the two disagree

Your page 7 warns that the "discover your WHY" fashion ignores the other four domains. Much of the web's best practice (Sinek) puts WHY first. Your method treats Purpose as one of five statements in balance.

Sources

Collins & Porras, Building Your Company's Vision, HBR 1996 Simon Sinek, Find Your Why Drucker Institute, Drucker on Mission

Best practice says

  • A few principles, five or six at most, that actually decide behaviour. More than that usually means practices or strategies mixed in (Collins & Porras).
  • The test: would we keep this value if it cost us? Real values "inflict pain" (Lencioni).
  • Separate true core values from aspirational ones, from permission-to-play ones such as honesty, which every business must meet anyway, and from accidental ones (Lencioni).
  • Built into hiring, reviews, promotion and dismissal. Avoid bland words: integrity, teamwork and customer focus appear on half the Fortune 100's lists, and on Enron's.

Your Alignment document says

Best practice says

  • The sources disagree most here, and fall into three camps.
  • Camp 1: mission means the reason the business exists, and it lasts forever. Drucker says it "should fit on a T-shirt"; Collis & Rukstad call it "why we exist".
  • Camp 2: mission means what we do, and for whom (Bain).
  • Camp 3: mission means a bold goal with a finish line and a date. Collins & Porras use NASA's moon landing as the example. Your document sits in this camp: one measurable milestone, a date, and a clear step towards the Vision.

How it differs from its neighbours

In your method, Mission is the next staging post, Vision is the destination, and Strategy is the route. Readers from camp 1 will take a milestone mission for a purpose statement, so it helps to say which kind this is in the plan.

Sources

Drucker Institute, Drucker on Mission Bain, Mission and Vision Statements Collins & Porras, Building Your Company's Vision, HBR 1996

Your Alignment document says

Speaking to Board Members and Owners: the over-arching philosophy about how you intend to achieve the Vision. It answers How, and delivers Leverage.

Best practice says

  • A short statement of choices, not a plan. Collis & Rukstad suggest 35 words or fewer covering three things: the objective (one measurable, time-bound goal), the scope (which customers, where, and what you will not do) and the advantage (why customers choose you, and what you do that others cannot combine).
  • Lafley & Martin put it as two choices at the heart: where to play and how to win.
  • Two tests: could a competitor say the same thing? Does it say what you will not do? A plan that never says what you won't do is not a strategy (Martin).

How it differs from its neighbours

Strategy is the one statement of the five expected to change as conditions change; Purpose and Values stay. Mission is the what-by-when; Strategy is the how.

Where the two disagree

Best practice puts the measurable objective inside the strategy; your document puts it in the Mission.

Sources

Collis & Rukstad, Can You Say What Your Strategy Is?, HBR 2008 Roger Martin, The Big Lie of Strategic Planning, HBR 2014 Roger Martin on strategy (Lenny's Newsletter)
