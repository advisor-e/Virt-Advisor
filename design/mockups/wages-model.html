🔴 Read this before the screens — what makes this model different

This is the only model in the library that would hold data about identifiable people rather than about a company. The workbook's sixth sheet lists a client's employees by name with their pay rate, sick leave taken, accrued leave, date of birth, age, and a rating of each one as Vital, Beneficial or Dispensable. Those people are not the client, they have consented to nothing, and they will never see the screen.

Mike's ruling of 2026-09-14 changes the position substantially, and it is worth stating why. In his words: "the page that describes dispensible etc relates to a due diligence process in the case of a company merging or acquring. Whilst the incoming owner must have regard for existing staff, the re structure may require tough decisions in places of 'role double up' so at some point, someone has to decide who stays and who goes. it is not for 'general application'."

  • A time-boxed judgement inside a transaction is defensible in a way a standing opinion is not. The same words — "this role is dispensable" — mean one thing in a dated restructure analysis prepared for an acquisition, and something quite different sitting permanently on a screen a manager can open on a Tuesday.
  • So the sheet is OFF by default and cannot be switched on at will — Decision 6. It opens only for a client whose case is in the app's existing due-diligence domain, and it carries the date and the transaction it was prepared for on its own face.
  • It is never sent to the AI. This whole model is arithmetic — its route calls no language model at all — so that holds by construction rather than by promise. The standing rule strips personal data before anything reaches a model, and the single exception ever granted is Meeting Review.
  • The rating is the advisor's, and the screen says so. Whatever it is finally called, it is recorded as a named person's judgement on a named date, not as a fact about an employee.

Decisions 6, 7 and 8 are where this is settled. Nothing about the other five sheets raises any of it — they are pay rates, hours and margins, and they are ordinary.

The decisions

Decision 1 — what it is called, and where it sits on the shelf

The workbook is Wages Model.xlsx, but "wages" undersells it: the screen's answer is labour margin — whether the team bills more than it costs — not a payroll total.

Recommended: name it Wages & Labour Margin, category Profitability, class Report.

Why Profitability. Its output is a margin and it sits naturally beside Margin · Mark-up · Break-even and 8 Levers. Why Report class — and this matters on screen: Report and Decision classes never carry the "Illustrative" badge, because the figures are a real client's. Getting this wrong would tell an advisor, in front of their client, that the client's own payroll is dummy data.

The card's summary line, for your wording: "What the team costs against what it bills, month by month — with the hiring plan and pay rises that move it."

Decision 2 — the workbook has TWO businesses in it, and they do not reconcile

This is the largest finding of reading it, and it is a real fork.

Sheet 1 — Hrly Rate & Tax CalculatorSheets 2–6 — the rest
15 people in 7 ROLES — Director, Consultant, Associate, Senior, Manager, Senior Tech, Junior Tech. A professional-services firm billing by job type. Nobody is named. 24 people in 4 DIVISIONS — Admin, Sales, Production, Management. A field-and-production business. Everybody is named.
Charge-out rates of $130–$375/hr across seven job types, with a mix percentage per role. Charge-out rates of $35–$65/hr, pay rates of $19–$77/hr.
Feeds nothing. No other sheet reads a single cell from it. Sheets 2, 3 and 4 all feed sheet 5, the Cash Report.

They are two different companies. Sheet 1 is a self-contained calculator that happens to share a workbook with the model; nothing links them.

Recommended: build both, as two tabs on one screen — "Labour margin" (sheets 2–6) and "Rates & tax" (sheet 1) — and say plainly on the second tab that its figures are its own and do not feed the first.

Why not split them into two library cards. Two cards means two entries, two routes, two sets of tests and a second thing for an advisor to find; and the rates calculator is too small to carry a card of its own. Why not drop sheet 1. The standing rule is never to remove an element of a source model without asking — and its tax and bonus maths is the most immediately useful thing in the workbook for an advisor sitting with a client.

Decision 3 — two operating bases, one switch

The workbook computes the whole year twice, on two different theories of what limits the work, and a Yes/No cell at Cash Report!C7 picks which one the report shows.

Decision 4 — the figures are typed, not imported

Nothing in a Balance Sheet or a Profit and Loss export carries a pay rate, a charge-out rate or a hiring plan. Those come from the client's payroll and from the advisor's conversation with them.

Recommended: typed entry, exactly like High-Level and Mid-Level Budget, with no file import in this first build.

A payroll import is a real future stage and a large one — every payroll system exports a different shape. It is worth doing later on its own merits, and it is not worth holding this model behind.

Decision 5 — the tax bands are the advisor's to type, and that is the argument for taking them off him

⚠ Corrected 2026-09-14. This box previously said sheet 1 "hardcodes the five New Zealand income-tax bands". It does not, and the truth makes the case stronger rather than weaker.

What is actually there. Every ceiling and every rate is a typed cell — $14,000 at 10.5%, $48,000 at 17.5%, $70,000 at 30%, $180,000 at 33%, 39% above. Only the band floors are calculated, each being the one below it plus a dollar. Beside them sit two spare custom band tables, labelled Custm 1 and Custm 2, and a State Taxes column under the note "Scroll right to view combined (concurrent) State & Federal Taxes".

So the workbook already anticipated this model crossing borders, and its answer was to let whoever opens it retype the bands. That is the problem, not the solution: it puts a tax table in every advisor's hands with nothing checking it, and a stale band is invisible — the arithmetic stays perfect while the answer goes wrong.

The app already solves it properly. There is a Tax Rates screen, and country rate schedules load at global group manager — one person loads every country their brand operates in, once.

Recommended: read the bands from the existing country tax-rate tables, keep the workbook's ability to override them for a one-off case, and where a country's bands have not been loaded say so and withhold the tax figure rather than computing a New Zealand answer for an Australian client.

This is the one place the model should not be a faithful port — and note what the recommendation keeps: the override survives, because the workbook's two custom tables prove somebody needed one. A wrong tax figure is exactly what UAT cannot catch; it looks perfectly plausible.

Decision 6 — RULED BY MIKE 2026-09-14 — the staff register opens only in a due-diligence project

Mike, 2026-09-14: "it is not for 'general application' so this page could be 'turned on' only in the cases of a firm being in a due diligence project?" — and the reason: "the re structure may require tough decisions in places of 'role double up' so at some point, someone has to decide who stays and who goes."

There is already a hook for this and it needs nothing new invented. data/domains.json carries a due-diligence domain — keywords acquisition, merger, buying a business — with three questions the Virtual Advisor already asks, including dueDiligenceScenario and dueDiligenceTimeline.

Recommended shape, drawn below: two conditions, both required.

ConditionWhy both
1. The client's case is in the due-diligence domain. This is the app's own record that a transaction is under way. It cannot be set by opening the wages screen.
2. The advisor switches the register on, and it records who and when. An automatic reveal is not a decision anyone made. The switch is what makes it a dated, attributable act — which is exactly what makes it defensible.

When the case leaves the due-diligence domain the register closes again and what was entered is not shown. It is not a permanent property of the client.

One thing this does NOT do, and should not: it does not stop an advisor writing the same judgement in a document of their own. It is not a control on the advisor. It is a refusal by our app to hold that material as ordinary standing data about a person.

Decision 7 — the words on the register, which are the workbook's and may not be yours

The workbook's three ratings are Vital, Beneficial, Dispensable, with its own definitions — "Immediate financial loss could be attributed to them leaving", "Financial loss could (indirectly) be attributed", "No (or very little) financial loss".

Note what those definitions actually say: every one of them is about financial loss to the business, not about the person. The words drifted from the definitions — "Dispensable" reads as a verdict on someone, where the definition underneath it is a measurement of revenue at risk.

Recommended: keep the workbook's three bands exactly as they are, and put the definition on the screen beside each one — but ask you for the three labels rather than shipping "Dispensable". A label like Revenue at risk: High / Indirect / Low says the same thing the definitions say.

This is your call and I have not assumed it. The drawing below shows the workbook's own words so you can see what the port would say if nothing changes.

Decision 9 — 1,778 typed cells, and how many of them reach a screen

⚠ Added 2026-09-14 on Mike's correction, which is what this decision exists to answer: "don't assume that things like 'wet n dark' days are a set figure - you will see this throughout the entire xisting model."

Classifying every cell gives the size of the problem. 1,778 typed against 8,714 calculated — and the typed ones are not evenly spread:

A single form cannot carry that, and a faithful port that tried would be unusable. Equally, quietly fixing any of them as a constant removes the control the model exists to give — which is precisely the fault Stock Purchasing hit, where five score ladders looked like fixed bands and were the owner's editable columns.

Recommended: a step per sheet, in the workbook's own order — People, Seasons or Shutdown, Hiring plan, then the report — with every typed cell reachable and none invented as a default we made up. Where a figure is genuinely the same for everyone, it is entered once at the top of its step rather than repeated per person.

The one thing not to do is ship the report screen first and add the inputs later. The numbers on it are worthless until the inputs behind them are the client's, and a screen showing the sample company's figures is how a demo becomes a wrong answer in front of a real client.

Decision 8 — what is stored, and the one number worth keeping

The register's whole content is personal data. The rest of the model is not.

Recommended: the register is held for the life of the due-diligence project and then deleted, on the same pattern Meeting Review already uses for transcripts — a promised period, and the data goes when it expires. The other five sheets store normally.

One figure should outlive it, and it belongs to the company rather than to anyone in it: the accrued annual leave liability, $63,154 in the sample. That is a real balance-sheet number an acquirer is buying, it is already in the client's own accounts, and it names nobody. It carries forward as a total; the names it was built from do not.

What building it involves

Honest sizing

So the scope is agreed before it starts, not discovered halfway.

PieceWhat it is
Maths enginePure backend module, Node 14 CommonJS. Two bases, per-person costing, hiring plan, rise schedule.
Golden testEvery expected figure carries its source cell. Written with the engine, never after.
RouteOne calc route — numbers in, numbers out, no AI, nothing stored.

The input screens🔴 Decision 9 — 1,778 typed cells reachable across a step per sheet. Added after Mike's correction; the first cut of this table did not have this row, which is how a model gets estimated at half its size.

This is several sessions, not one — and more of them than the first cut of this page implied. Six sheets and ~10,500 filled cells make it the largest port in the library; for comparison, the Sales Dashboard built in a day had 140 rows and one output table. The correction about input cells moves the weight of the work: the engine is the smaller half, and getting 1,778 typed figures onto screens an advisor can actually work through is the larger one. A sensible order is the labour-margin engine and its golden test first, because everything else depends on those numbers being right; then the input steps; then the report screen; then the rates tab; and the gated register last, once its three decisions are settled.
