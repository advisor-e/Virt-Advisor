What the screen does today — measured against your spreadsheet

  • 49 boxes, and 32 of them come from a column that is empty from top to bottom in your sheet and carries no heading. An advisor is handed 32 places to type that correspond to nothing in your document.
  • 42 boxes are labelled only with a row number — 1.0, 2.0, on to 30.0 — and 7 carry no label at all. Nothing on the screen says which box is a job title and which is who that person reports to.

☑ Why this one is not a form — your ruling, 2026‑09‑21

“seeing as this needs to be dynamic — perhaps it needs to be built like a ‘mini-app’ in order to function? … which is why the original is in a spreadsheet”

  • Your file types say it. Of the 20 templates you supplied, 17 are Word documents and only 2 are spreadsheets — this is one of them. A Word document is a form with a fixed number of blanks. You reached for a spreadsheet because an org chart is a list that grows, with a relationship between two of its columns.
  • Every other capture form saves a box by its position in your table — table 0, row 5, column 3 — and the save is checked against exactly the positions your document contains. Your sheet has 30 numbered rows. An advisor adding role 31 would save to a position your sheet does not have, and the save would be refused. A fixed grid does not merely look wrong here; it breaks at the 31st name.
  • One answer is constrained by another. “Reporting Head” has to name a role that already exists. No other form has a field whose valid answers depend on what was typed in a different row.
  • It can be wrong in ways a text box cannot notice — two people reporting to each other, two chief executives, somebody reporting to nobody.
  • And it produces something. The other eight capture words. This one captures a structure, from which the chart your deck teaches can be drawn.

3 · The chart, drawn from that list as it is typed

Nothing here is entered twice. Every box and every line below is computed from the 24 rows above · 7 levels · the panel scrolls sideways

● A notice, never a block. Your own example raises one: Board reports to Shareholders, and Shareholders is not one of the 24 roles — so it is drawn above the chart as an outside parent rather than treated as an error. The same applies to two people reporting to each other or to a second chief executive: the screen says so and carries on. This follows your ruling of 2026‑09‑08 that a check “never blocks the advisor”, and it is not a decision below.

The rule this form follows

  • A row is a person, not a position on a page. Rows are added and removed, and the number on the left is worked out rather than typed.
  • A column that names another row is a picker, never free text. It offers the roles already entered, and never the role in its own row.
  • A column blank in its header and blank in every row is a gutter, not part of the form — the rule you approved for the two-dimensional grid. Your sheet has 1.
  • A structure that cannot be right is flagged and never blocked, and what the advisor typed is always kept.

A · Does the screen open empty, or with your 24 roles already in it?

On Customer Types you ruled that your worked column arrives filled in and is typed over. That was one column. Here your example is an entire company’s chart — 24 roles from Board to Personal Assistant.

Recommended: open empty, with Start from the example loading all 24 in one click. An org chart is the client’s own, so roles that are not theirs are 24 deletions before they can begin — where Customer Types was one column to type over. The example is still one click away, so it does the job your worked examples exist to do.

Against: it is one more click, and an advisor who does not press it never sees your example at all.

☑ RULED: the screen opens EMPTY, with Start from the example loading all 24 roles in one click. Mike, 2026‑09‑21 — “yes - empty”. As recommended.

B · Your Role column has no heading. Do we give it one?

Your sheet names the third column “Reporting Head” and leaves the second one unheaded. On 2026‑09‑21 you ruled that Operational Objectives’ unheaded column stays unheaded, because a heading we invent is indistinguishable from yours once it is on the page. This case differs in one way: there the whole row was unheaded, so nothing looked missing; here one column is named and the one beside it is not.

Recommended: head it Role. It is the word your own Design the Organisational Hierarchy Chart and your summary “Who reports to who?” already use, and a blank heading beside a filled one reads as a fault rather than as your document.

Against: it is a word of ours on a screen where every other word is yours, and your 2026‑09‑21 ruling points the other way.

☑ RULED: the column is headed Role. Mike, 2026‑09‑21. As recommended. ⚠ This is the ONE word on this screen that is ours rather than his — recorded here so it is never mistaken for his document.

C · Your empty fourth column — 32 of today’s 49 boxes

Your sheet has a fourth column with no heading and nothing in it in any of its 32 rows. Today it becomes 32 boxes to type into.

Recommended: drop it, so an advisor types a role and who it reports to and nothing else. Under the rule you approved for the two-dimensional grid it is a gutter, not part of the form.

Against: it is your spreadsheet, and you may have left it there as room for a note against each person.

☑ RULED: the empty fourth column is DROPPED. An advisor types a role and who it reports to, and nothing else. Mike, 2026‑09‑21. As recommended. This alone removes 32 of today’s 49 boxes.

D · The number down the left — typed, or worked out?

Your sheet numbers the rows 1.0 to 30.0. Once roles can be added and removed, a number that was typed goes stale the first time somebody deletes a row above it.

Recommended: the app keeps the number and renumbers on every change; the advisor never types it. A stale number in a client’s document is a small error that looks like carelessness.

Against: your sheet numbers in tenths (1.0), which allows a role to be inserted between two others without renumbering, and a computed number loses that.

☑ RULED: the app works out the number and renumbers on every change. The advisor never types it. Mike, 2026‑09‑21. As recommended. ⚠ His tenths (1.0, 2.0) are therefore NOT carried across — a role is inserted by adding it and choosing who it reports to.

E · What does the client’s plan carry — the chart, the list, or both?

Every other capture form prints what the advisor typed. This one can print a drawn chart as well, because the structure is captured rather than described.

Recommended: the chart, with the list beneath it. The chart is the thing your deck teaches and the thing a client recognises; the list beneath it is what makes the chart checkable and survives being read aloud or printed in black and white.

Against: a wide chart on a printed page shrinks, and a 7-level chart is already 1628px wide on screen.

☑ RULED: the client’s plan carries the drawn chart, with the list of roles beneath it. Mike, 2026‑09‑21. As recommended. ⚠ The chart must therefore survive being printed: at 7 levels his own example is 1628px wide, so the plan page has to scale it rather than crop it.
