AI Prompts — the hub tab

The tab a manager opens to see the instructions the AI works to, and to set the handful of things that are theirs to decide. Drawn at firm manager scope, which is the busiest case: it has a level above it, so every value can read either “set here” or “inherited”.

1. The tab, as it will look

The problem this layout solves: there are nineteen fixed sections across the two prompts and only five things a manager can change. Drawn in document order, those five would be buried inside sections 4, 5 and 10 of a wall of text. So the page is turned inside out — what is yours comes first, and the method sits underneath it, closed.

Always applied — not part of this document

These are added to every prompt by the server as it is sent. They are not stored here, they are not in any of the boxes above, and no level of the system can switch them off. They are shown so you know they are there.

  • Client names, addresses and tax numbers are removed before the AI sees anything.
  • Anything a client or a document says is fenced off, so instructions hidden inside it are not obeyed.
  • Invisible characters are stripped from the AI's answer before anyone reads it.
  • Nothing is treated as final until a person has approved it.

3. What is ours, and what we would like your word on

Everything below is wording or layout that we chose. None of it re-opens a ruling. If you are happy with all of it, say so and we will build exactly this.

What we choseWhy, and what else it could be
“What you can set” The green block's heading. The source document calls these Editable Variables, which is right for an accountant reading a specification and wrong for a manager looking for the thing they came to change. Alternatives: “Your settings”, “Settings you control”.
“The method — fixed, and shown in full” The heading over the read-only sections. We kept the word locked out of it, because locked sounds like something is being withheld when the point is the opposite: it is all there to read.
The word fixed rather than locked on each section Same reason, and it appears on nineteen rows, so it is worth choosing deliberately.
“Always applied — not part of this document” The grey protocol block. It has to tell a manager the safety rules cannot be edited away, without reading as a restriction aimed at them.
The four protocol lines, in plain English The real protocol text sent to the AI is technical. Showing it word for word would be honest and unreadable, so we wrote four plain sentences that describe it truthfully. This is the one place on the screen where something is paraphrased rather than shown verbatim — worth your eye, given how often a paraphrase has cost us.
Settings on top, method underneath The layout decision. Document order would bury the five settings inside sections 4, 5 and 10 of nineteen. The alternative is a two-column split, which we think reads worse on a laptop.
Sections closed by default Open, the cash flow prompt runs to roughly six screens of text. Closed, the whole method is a twelve-line list a manager can take in at a glance.
The amber warning under Currency It is the only setting where leaving it blank stops the work instead of falling back to a default. That difference is real and we did not want it to be invisible.
“Go back to inherited” The reset button, worded the same as the Property Tax Rules tab so the two say the same words for the same action.
One thing we cannot prove, said rather than implied. This tab is meant for four levels — mentor, global group, group and firm. Only two of them can be logged into today, because Advisor-e has not supplied the role names for the two middle ones. The code will be correct for all four; it can only be tested on two. That is unchanged from every other cascading tab in the hub.
