How your clients’ information is protected

These are applied by the system every time, on every prompt. They are not stored on this page, they are not in any of the boxes above, and no level of the system can switch them off. They are here so you know they are there.

  • Client names, addresses and tax numbers are removed before the AI sees anything.
  • Anything a client or a document says is fenced off, so instructions hidden inside it are not obeyed.
  • Invisible characters are stripped from the AI’s answer before anyone reads it.
  • Pictures and web code are stripped out of the AI’s answer, so nothing can be hidden inside it.

Nothing here is yours to set

This prompt is entirely safety protocol. There is no part of it a firm, a group or a brand could sensibly change, so there are no boxes on it — and that is the right answer rather than something missing.

Nothing is hidden from a manager by this, and that is checkable. This prompt had no editable setting at any tier before the change and has none now, so no manager lost a control. 3. What changed from the 21 August drawing, and what is still ours

The first rows are your rulings. Everything after them is wording or layout that we chose. None of it re-opens a ruling. If you are happy with all of it, this is exactly what has been built.

What changedWhy, and what it cost
The security prompt is now mentor-only Your ruling, 22 August. Its seven headings — the lethal trifecta, gate the sinks, not the reads, taint-gate memory writes — were 7 of the 19 sections a firm manager saw, in a different profession’s language. That does the opposite of reassuring: it is a list of alarming things they cannot evaluate. What it cost: nothing a manager could act on. The prompt had no editable setting at any tier, so nothing was taken away from anyone — only a document they could not use.
Seven headings became four sentences Your ruling, 22 August. The panel How your clients’ information is protected replaces them below the mentor. ⚠ It is the one paraphrase on the screen — every other word the tab shows is the verbatim text the AI is sent. Given how often a paraphrase has cost us, it does not sit loose: each sentence is stored beside the protocol it describes and is tied by a test to the code that performs it, so a sentence whose protection is removed fails the build instead of going quietly false.
“Reporting periods”, and “Currency and units” kept Your word, 22 August. Setting 2 was Reporting granularity — the source document’s term, and a specification writer’s rather than an accountant’s. You kept Currency and units as it was.
The two-card picker is gone below the mentor Ours, though it follows from your rulings rather than being a fresh decision: a manager now has one document, and a picker offering a choice of one is furniture. The mentor keeps it, because the mentor genuinely has two.
The fourth safety sentence was replaced before it shipped Ours, and it is the same fault you caught on 22 August, in prose instead of in a control. The panel says these things are applied by the system every time. Three of the four are: names stripped, content fenced, invisible characters removed — all code, all tested. The fourth was “Nothing is treated as final until a person has approved it”, which is not enforced anywhere; it restates something the AI is asked to do. Under that heading it would have been a promise the app is not keeping. It now reads “Pictures and web code are stripped out of the AI’s answer”, which is enforced by the locked markdown pipeline and is the same kind of thing as the other three.
One safety panel, not two Ours, and the judgement worth your eye. The first drawing had a grey Always applied — not part of this document block AND the security prompt. Keeping both would have left a manager with two boxes answering the same question — is my client’s data safe? — which is the complication you objected to. They are now one panel. If you would rather the “cannot be switched off” point stood on its own, that is a five-minute change.
“What you can set” The green block’s heading, unchanged from the first drawing. The source document calls these Editable Variables, which is right for an accountant reading a specification and wrong for a manager looking for the thing they came to change.
“The method — fixed, and shown in full” The heading over the read-only sections, and the word fixed rather than locked on each row. Locked sounds like something is being withheld when the point is the opposite: it is all there to read.
Settings on top, method underneath Document order would bury the three settings inside sections 4, 5 and 10. Sections are closed by default: open, the prompt runs to roughly six screens; closed, the whole method is a twelve-line list a manager can take in at a glance.
The amber warning under Currency It is the only setting where leaving it blank stops the work instead of falling back to a default. That difference is real and we did not want it to be invisible.
“Go back to inherited” The reset button, worded the same as the Property Tax Rules tab so the two say the same words for the same action.

One thing we cannot prove, said rather than implied. This tab is meant for four levels — mentor, global group, group and firm. Only two of them can be logged into today, because Advisor-e has not supplied the role names for the two middle ones. The code is correct for all four; it can only be exercised on two. That is unchanged from every other cascading tab in the hub, and it is Advisor-e’s data to supply, not something we can finish here.
