Four things this screen does, and why each is here rather than invented.
  • Every point says where it came from — three sources today (From Advisor-e, From your firm, Added by you), and the middle tiers collapse into From your firm rather than naming a brand or a country an advisor has never heard of. That is question 2.
  • A switched-off point is shown, not hidden. The manager's screen already works this way, and the reason is written in its template: "a manager who cannot see what they turned off cannot turn it back on, and would read the shorter list as the whole list." The same holds for an advisor.
  • Switching off binds this advisor only. The firm's list is untouched, and the label says off for you only so that is visible rather than assumed.
  • Only a point you added can be edited or removed. Editing an inherited point's wording would be editing the firm's words — the same reason the manager screens offer Use the inherited wording instead of a free edit above their level.

P14 runs downward only, and a manager overriding an advisor's own level would be the first thing in this app to reach across it. If a firm wants a point back, that is a conversation, and now they can have it.
  • No threshold, no anonymity floor. The meeting aggregate has one (5 advisors, 20 meetings) because it reports on client meetings. This reports on configuration — who set what — so P13 is not engaged and a floor would only hide the single advisor a manager most needs to talk to.

Six questions

All six ruled 2026-09-08 · two changed the drawing
Question 1 — ✅ RULED BY MIKE, 2026-09-08

May an advisor switch off a point their firm set?

P14 permits it: an advisor's own level is below the firm's, and switching a point off there changes that advisor's list and nothing else. But permission is not the same as wisdom. A firm sets what its advisors are checked on; if each advisor may quietly drop the ones they find awkward, the firm's standard becomes a suggestion — and the manager's aggregate, which counts how the firm is doing against those points, starts measuring a list that differs per advisor.

✅ RULED — YES, any inherited point, as recommended. It is what P14 already says, it is how every other level in this app works, and the alternative — advisors may add but not remove — makes the advisor's level the only one in the cascade that behaves differently, which nobody would be able to predict.
🔴 AND THE ARGUMENT AGAINST WAS NOT ACCEPTED AS A COST — IT WAS ORDERED FIXED. His words: "yes but fix the issue - build it so the manager can see". The recorded cost was that no firm manager can see what advisors have switched off. That is no longer a cost carried by this slice; it is Stage C below, and it ships in the same piece of work. What he refused was not the permission but a permission whose consequence nobody could see.
Question 2 — ✅ RULED BY MIKE, 2026-09-08

Does the manager see who set a point aside, or only how many?

Stage C can name the four advisors, or it can say 4 of 12 and stop.

✅ RULED — NAME THEM, as recommended. A count tells a manager a problem exists and not who to talk to — which is the only reason they opened the screen. Their manager already sees them named on the Team tab, so nothing is disclosed here that was private before, and this is configuration rather than anything from a client meeting, so P13 and the aggregate's anonymity floor are not engaged.
Against: a named list of who has stepped outside the firm's standard is a small performance record, and it will be read as one. An advisor who set a point aside for a good reason — it does not fit the clients they handle — has no way to say so on this screen, and appears beside three who simply found it awkward.
Question 3 — ✅ RULED BY MIKE, 2026-09-08

Do the two middle tiers get their own source label?

A point can come from the mentor, a global group manager, a group manager, or the firm. The drawing collapses the middle two into From your firm.

✅ RULED — COLLAPSE THEM into From your firm, as recommended. An advisor has no relationship with a global group manager or a group manager, and would read "From the UK group" as a question rather than an answer. Everything above them that is not Advisor-e is, from where they sit, their firm.
Against: a firm manager told "my advisors see it as ours" about a point they cannot edit may reasonably object. The alternative is From your group as a third label.
Question 4 — ✅ RULED BY MIKE, 2026-09-08

Is the source shown on every point, or only where it is not the firm's?

✅ RULED — EVERY POINT, ALWAYS, as recommended. A label that appears only sometimes teaches an advisor to read its absence as meaning something, and they will guess wrong. It is also what D1 asks for in terms.
Against: it puts a line of grey text under all eleven points in some meeting types, on a screen whose whole job is to be read quickly before walking into a room.
Question 5 — 🔴 RULED AGAINST THE RECOMMENDATION, 2026-09-08

May an advisor set the "words that hint it happened" on their own point?

Managers can. Those words tell the model what to listen for in the transcript.

Recommended: NO, withhold them — and Mike ruled the other way. The recommendation was that an advisor tuning the phrases the AI looks for in their own assessment is marking their own homework, and the coaching notes are what a manager may later read.
🔴 RULED — NO, DO NOT WITHHOLD. The advisor gets the hint words. This is the one ruling on this page that REPLACED the recommendation, and the argument it accepted was the one recorded against it: a point the advisor wrote themselves is the one the model is least likely to recognise, because it is phrased in their words and nobody else's. Withholding the hints would have made an advisor's own additions the weakest entries on their own list — a strange reward for the only person who bothered to personalise it. So the add form has TWO fields, not one, and screen B3 above is drawn to the ruling rather than to the recommendation.
The cost, carried rather than solved. The marking-your-own-homework objection did not stop being true; it was outweighed. An advisor can now phrase both the point and what the AI listens for, and a manager reading the coaching notes cannot see that they did. If that ever matters, the fix is to show the hint words' source on the manager's side — not to take the field away again.
Question 6 — ✅ RULED BY MIKE, 2026-09-08

Where does this live — the pre-set screen itself, or a settings page of its own?

✅ RULED — THE PRE-SET SCREEN ITSELF, as recommended and as drawn. It is where an advisor already reads the list, so the moment they think "that one is not how I work" is the moment the control is under their thumb. A separate page means remembering it exists.
Against: the pre-set is read in the two minutes before a client meeting, and putting editing controls there invites fiddling at exactly the wrong moment. A quieter screen would keep the pre-set purely for reading.

🔴 The per-CLIENT half of slice 4 is deliberately NOT drawn here. D2 gives the advisor and the firm manager a per-client level — "how I run meetings with this client" — and it is real work with its own screen. It is left out for one reason: it hangs off the client picker, and that picker is empty without MySQL, which this laptop does not have. Drawing a screen nobody can then verify is how a feature ends up built and unproven. It gets its own drawing when it can be run.

What this drawing does not solve. An advisor has no way to say why they set a point aside, so Stage C shows a manager the fact and never the reason — question 2's recorded cost, and the obvious next thing if that turns out to sting. The reference-material join — the firm's script one tap away from a point — remains the largest unbuilt piece of Meeting Review and is untouched by this. And nothing here writes any advisory content: the points an advisor adds are theirs, and the eleven meeting types still need their observation points written by Mike.
